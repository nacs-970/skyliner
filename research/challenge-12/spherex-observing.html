How SPHEREx takes its snapshot of the sky

Not random, and not a fixed list typed in by hand: a planner picks pointings from a pre-made list, one orbit at a time, so that in about six months every spot on the sky has been seen at all 102 colours. Below, each step, with the real exposure times of our WISE 0855 images as a check.

1 · Where does it point?

A plan, not a free choice: a ring 90° from the Sun that turns once a year

SPHEREx sits in a 700 km orbit over the poles that follows the day-night line (a "dawn-dusk" orbit), and it never looks closer than 91° to the Sun. So its view stays on a great circle 90° from the Sun. As the Earth goes round the Sun, that circle turns, and in about six months it has swept over the whole sky. Any given star lies on the ring twice a year, about six months apart.

Which exact pointing comes next is chosen by survey planning software from a main target list. It looks at the pointing zone left by the Sun and Earth limits, the orbit, and what the mission has already covered.

Top view of the ecliptic plane, schematic. The orange rays are where the viewing ring cuts the plane. The strip below the slider shows when our SPHEREx images of WISE 0855 were really taken (green), and the dates when the computed Sun–target angle is exactly 90° (dashed). They line up.

2 · What is one exposure?

One 112-second stare gives six images, and each pixel has its own colour

  • A 20 cm telescope feeds six detector arrays in two groups of three. A dichroic mirror splits the light, so both groups look at the same patch of sky at the same moment, one in short wavelengths (arrays D1 to D3, 0.75 to 2.44 µm) and one in long (D4 to D6, 2.40 to 5.01 µm).
  • Each array sees 3.5° × 3.5° (about 2040 × 2040 pixels of 6.15″). One group of three covers 3.5° × 11.3°.
  • On top of each array sits a linear variable filter: its colour changes smoothly from one side to the other. So the wavelength a star is measured at depends on where on the array it lands.
  • To measure a star at a new colour, the telescope moves it along the filter: a step of about 12′ (about 117 pixels) moves it to the next of 17 channels per array.

3 · What happens in one orbit?

Bursts of up to four exposures, then a big slew

One orbit takes about computedfor 700 km altitude. The orbit is cut into segments of about 10 minutes. At the start of a segment the telescope turns to the planned target and stares for about 112 s. Then it steps about 12′ (next colour) and stares again, up to 4 exposures per segment. Then the Earth limits force a big slew of about 60° to a new patch for the next segment.

4 · How does a spectrum appear?

Never in one go: the same spot is seen again and again, at new colours

A single exposure measures a star at one wavelength per array. The spectrum is built from many exposures, as the pointing moves the star along the filter and the strip comes round again over several days. Each dot below is one real image of WISE 0855; its position is the wavelength the target was measured at.

One detector's range is covered by about 17 channels (the mission paper's "minimum of four spectra at every point along the ecliptic" comes from four full surveys, each offset by half a channel). Gaps in a row mean the target was not on that part of the filter in the observed windows.

5 · Where does "six months" come from?

One survey pass is the ring sweeping the whole sky once

The ring covers the sky in about 6 months, and the baseline plan is four full surveys in two years. For one star, that means a visit near the date the ring passes it, then another roughly six months later, and so on. Between those windows the telescope is looking at other parts of the sky, so for our target there is simply no observation.

The "snapshot of six months" is not one picture. It is a collection of many exposures taken over months, where each position got a few windows of days, split by long gaps.

6 · Why this matters for the tool

What the flipbook can and cannot show

  • Frames inside a window come minutes to hours apart, but each is at a different wavelength. A change between neighbours can be a different colour, not a different time.
  • Windows are weeks to months apart, so slow motion shows up as a jump between windows. For WISE 0855 the move is only about one pixel a year.
  • The gaps are real. They are why the timeline lets you select a time with no observation.
  • New releases add observations as later surveys cover the same spot, which is why the tool should be easy to update.
