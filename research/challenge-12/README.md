# Challenge 12: open research questions

Questions that the tool design depends on. They come from the "Not tested" list in [../07-spherex-data-check.md](../07-spherex-data-check.md) and from NASA's idea list in [../02-challenges-2026.md](../02-challenges-2026.md).

Run the data-access tests (the first group) first; the hackathon has started. Record each answer below the question, with the date and the exact URL or call used.

Findings so far are in [data-findings.md](data-findings.md): the data layers, the FITS structure, time coverage and release cadence, problems that come from the data alone, and a comparison with `research/demo/`.

## Data access (hour 0)

- [x] Do the IRSA SIA2 endpoint and the cutout service send CORS headers, so a browser page can call them directly? This decides stack Option A or B.
  - Answered (14 Nov): no. SIA2, TAP, the cutout service and the `ibe` file server send no CORS headers. The public S3 bucket does (see Answers).
- [x] What are the cutout service parameters (position, size, format, which extensions)? How large is one cutout, and how long does one take?
  - Answered (14 Nov): `center` and `size`, with units. A cutout holds every extension and is about 4 MB however small it is, and takes 10 to 17 s (see Answers).
- [ ] Can we request many cutouts at once, or only one at a time? Are there rate limits?
  - Partly answered (14 Nov): one cutout per request. We ran 2 to 3 in parallel for the whole year (260 cutouts) with occasional HTTP 503 and no block. No published limit found, and we did not probe for one.
- [x] Do astroquery or pyvo calls work against the current collection names (`spherex_dr1`, `spherex_qr3`)? Do we need them, or are plain HTTP and astropy enough?
  - Answered (14 Nov): `Irsa.query_sia` and `Irsa.query_tap` work for `spherex_dr1`. Plain HTTP plus astropy is enough and has fewer dependencies (see Answers).
- [x] What are the TAP table names, and does TAP give anything SIA2 does not?
  - Answered (14 Nov): `spherex.obscore`, `spherex.observation`, `spherex.plane`, `spherex.artifact`, `spherex.provenance_input`. TAP adds SQL filters and exact `s_region` containment, but a query took 19 s against 6 s for SIA2 (see Answers).
- [x] Does the S3 bucket listing work without an account, and is it faster than the cutout service?
  - Answered (14 Nov): yes, it works, and HTTP range reads of one frame took 3.7 s for about 1 MB, against 17 s and 4 MB for the cutout service. It also sends CORS headers (see Answers).

## Science and data content

- [ ] How are images spread in time for one position? Is the `t_min` spacing enough to see motion of a nearby brown dwarf like WISE 0855?
  - Partly answered (14 Nov): WISE 0855 in `spherex_dr1` has 143 exposures in 4 windows over 374 days, with gaps of months. At an assumed 8 arcseconds per year it moves only about 1.3 pixels over that baseline. Other targets are not analysed. See section 4 of [data-findings.md](data-findings.md).
- [ ] What do the FLAGS extension values mean, and which ones mark contamination worth showing?
  - Partly answered (14 Nov): 8 of the 12 bits set in one frame are identified. Table 10 of the Explanatory Supplement has the full definitions and was not read (see Answers).
- [ ] How do the six detector arrays (D1 to D6) and the 102 bands map onto "one frame per time"? Which band or sum gives the best signal-to-noise for a flipbook?
  - Partly answered (14 Nov): one image comes from one detector and covers that detector's wavelength range, and a position gets about 2 images per exposure, so frames at the same time differ in wavelength. The `WCS-WAVE` contents are now known: wavelength changes across one image, by about 0.6 µm on D6 (see Answers). Signal-to-noise per band is not examined. See sections 3 and 4 of [data-findings.md](data-findings.md).
- [ ] What are the exact catalog positions for Barnard's Star, Epsilon Indi B, RW Aurigae and R Draconis? (Only WISE 0855 has an official position in the challenge text.)
- [ ] Does the IRSA Moving Object Search (MOST) tool help, or does it overlap with what we build?

## Answers

- **2026-11-13 and 2026-11-14:** read the SIA2 search result and one FITS file's headers and pixels, and measured time coverage and release dates for WISE 0855 (`spherex_dr1`) and Barnard's Star (`spherex_qr3`). Results in [data-findings.md](data-findings.md). Not answered by it: CORS, cutout service behavior, `FLAGS` bit meanings, real proper motions, and the other test targets.

- **2026-11-14, data-access tests.** All calls used the generic User-Agent `sphereX-cache-demo/0.1`, and `Origin: https://example.org` where CORS was tested. The frame used is the one full frame we already downloaded (`2025W19_1B_0116_1`, detector D6).
  - **CORS.** A GET with an `Origin` header got no `Access-Control-Allow-Origin` from SIA2 (`https://irsa.ipac.caltech.edu/SIA`), TAP, the cutout service or a HEAD on the `ibe` file URL. A preflight `OPTIONS` to the cutout service returns HTTP 405 (`allow: GET`). So a browser cannot call any of them directly. The public S3 bucket `nasa-irsa-spherex` answers with `Access-Control-Allow-Origin: *`, methods `HEAD, GET`, and `Access-Control-Allow-Headers: range` on the preflight, so a browser can read it, including byte ranges.
  - **S3 bucket.** Listing works without an account (`https://nasa-irsa-spherex.s3.amazonaws.com/?list-type=2&prefix=dr1/&delimiter=/`). Top-level folders are `dr1/`, `qr2/` and `qr3/`. `dr1/level2/<week>/<version>/<detector number>/level2_<obs_id>D<n>_spx_<version>.fits` holds the same file (same name, same 65,672,640 bytes) as the `ibe` path, and the files are uncompressed. The extensions sit at fixed byte offsets (from the astropy file info: IMAGE header at 2,880 and data at 25,920, 16.6 MB, 2040 by 2040 float32, one 8,160-byte row at a time). Two range requests, the IMAGE header and 117 whole rows (954,720 bytes), came back as HTTP 206 in 1.3 s and 2.4 s, and both were byte-identical to our local copy. This is a partial read, not a full-frame download, but it is a different route from the cutout service that `AGENTS.md` names, so the team should decide whether to allow it. The catch: S3 has no spatial index, so finding which files cover a position still needs SIA2 or TAP, and those have no CORS.
  - **Cutout service.** `<access_url>?center=RA,DEC&size=DEG` as before. The error text lists the accepted units for `center` and `size`: pixels (`px`), arcsec, arcmin, deg and rad. A cutout of `size=0.05` (about 29 pixels) was still 3,962,880 bytes and took 17.5 s, because the `EPSF` table dominates, so a smaller field does not save bytes. A center outside the image returns HTTP 422 `Arrays do not overlap.` in 0.9 s. Bad parameters return HTTP 422 with a readable message. A partial overlap at the edge returns a smaller array (4 of our 260 cutouts, which the export drops).
  - **astroquery and pyvo.** astroquery 0.4.11 and pyvo 1.10.0. `Irsa.query_sia(pos=(coord, 0.02 deg), collection="spherex_dr1")` returned 260 rows for WISE 0855 in 5.7 s, the same count as our own SIA2 call. `Irsa.query_tap(...)` worked. `Irsa.list_collections()` lists all IRSA collections. We do not need them, because plain HTTP and astropy do the same with fewer dependencies.
  - **TAP.** `https://irsa.ipac.caltech.edu/TAP/tables` lists `spherex.obscore`, `spherex.observation`, `spherex.plane`, `spherex.artifact` and `spherex.provenance_input`. `spherex.obscore` has `obs_id`, `obs_collection`, `s_ra`, `s_dec`, `s_region` (a polygon), `t_min`, `t_max`, `t_exptime`, `em_min`, `em_max`, `access_url`, `s_pixel_scale` and `obs_release_date`, among others. A `CONTAINS(POINT(...), s_region)` count for WISE 0855 gave 258 rows against 260 for the SIA2 circle (the circle has a 0.02 degree radius, so it also catches images that only touch it). It took 18.7 s, so TAP is slower than SIA2 for this.
  - **FLAGS.** The extension is `int32`, mask type `MASK`, with the same WCS as IMAGE. The IMAGE header carries per-flag pixel counts (`N_TRANSIENT`, `N_COLD`, ...), and matching them to the bit counts in the FLAGS array identifies these bits: 0 `TRANSIENT` (22,478 px), 11 `COLD` (2,608), 17 `PERSIST` (209), 19 `OUTLIER` (54,066) and 21 `SOURCE` (1,613,409, 38.8% of the frame, so `SOURCE` is not a defect and must not be shown as contamination). The Spectrophotometry Tool page names bit 2 `SUR_ERROR`, bit 6 `NONFUNC` and bit 17 `PERSIST`, which agrees with the count match. Bit 26 (67 px) is probably `SNOW` (`N_SNOW_NS` is 68, and it excludes `SOURCE` pixels), not certain. Bits 1, 12, 27 and 28 are not identified. 59.0% of the pixels are unflagged. Full definitions are in Table 10 of the Explanatory Supplement (<https://irsa.ipac.caltech.edu/data/SPHEREx/docs/SPHEREx_Expsupp_QR.pdf>), which we did not read.
  - **WCS-WAVE.** A one-row binary table with `X` and `Y` (9 pixel positions each: 1, 256, 511, 766, 1021, 1275, 1530, 1785, 2040) and `VALUES`, a 9 by 9 grid of two numbers: the central wavelength and the bandwidth in micrometres. It is a lookup table for the linear variable filter. On D6 the wavelength runs from 5.02 µm at the first row down to 4.40 µm at the last, and hardly changes along x. The bandwidth is 0.034 to 0.039 µm. So one 117-pixel cutout spans about 0.036 µm, one channel, which matches the step of about 0.035 µm between exposures in a burst. At our target pixel the table gives about 4.97 µm, and the SIA2-based index gives 4.9685 µm for this frame.
  - **Not answered:** rate limits (not probed), the exact meaning of FLAGS bits 1, 12, 26, 27 and 28, and whether the `qr2` and `qr3` folders follow the same layout as `dr1`.

- **2026-11-14, recheck of the Python service method.** Two things were checked: how long a cold request really takes, and how the S3 range read behaves from this machine.
  - **Cold request time through the cutout service.** The pre-warm in [../../docs/handoff.md](../../docs/handoff.md) section 6 fetched 208 D6 cutouts in about 90 minutes with 2 parallel requests and about 130 HTTP 503 responses. That is about 26 seconds of wall time per cutout, not the 10 to 17 seconds one request takes. At that rate one detector of one target (38 to 70 images) takes about 17 to 30 minutes, and all six detectors (227 to 427 DR1 images per target, see the probe counts in the handoff) take about 1.7 to 3 hours. The handoff's "a cold request takes minutes" holds for one detector at best, and then only for tens of minutes. A visitor who types an uncached position cannot wait for that, so the page needs a queue, a progress state and an honest time estimate.
  - **S3 range read, measured from this machine** (one frame, `2025W19_1B_0116_1D6`, generic User-Agent, no frame stored). `HEAD` returned HTTP 200, `Accept-Ranges: bytes`, `Content-Length: 65672640` and `Access-Control-Allow-Origin: *`. One range of 117 image rows (954,720 bytes) came back as HTTP 206 in 2.3 s. Four of the same request in parallel came back in 2.3, 2.4, 5.1 and 5.9 s, so the total was roughly 0.4 to 0.7 MB/s and parallel requests did not add speed. This is not the local link: an 8 MB download from a nearby CDN ran at 2.2 MB/s. See the next bullet for the likely cause.
  - **Why the downloads are slow (measured from Thailand, later on 14 Nov).** One cutout request to the IRSA cutout service gave the first byte after 4.9 s (the server building the cutout), then 4,170,240 bytes in 19.6 s, which is 0.21 MB/s (24.5 s in all). The request just before it got HTTP 503 after 1.0 s. The connection time to both IRSA and the S3 bucket is about 0.27 s, so both are far away (the United States). The two US hosts gave 0.2 to 0.5 MB/s per stream, against 2.2 MB/s from a nearby CDN. The likely cause is a long network path, not the servers' bandwidth, but one measurement cannot prove it. If it is right, a service hosted in the United States would fetch the same data much faster than this laptop. The pre-warm's wall time (about 26 s per cutout) is probably made of three parts: the server's cutout build (about 5 s), the slow transfer (about 20 s of a 4 MB file), and the sleeps after HTTP 503 (10, 40, 90, 160, then 240 s each, in `http_get`). The split between them is an estimate, not a measurement.
  - **A second run, and why it is not a test of another region.** A cloud agent was asked to repeat the timings "from a US host". Its report shows the same kernel (`Linux 7.3.4-arch1-1`), a Thai-locale clock and the same RTK wrapper as this laptop, and its connect times (IRSA 0.23 s, S3 0.26 s) match ours, so it ran on this machine and network. It is a repeat, not an independent location, and the region question is still open. The repeat gave: S3 range read 954,720 bytes in 2.27 s (earlier 2.3 s); CDN 8 MB at 2.85 MB/s; a cutout whose first byte came after 0.8 s (earlier 4.9 s, so the server's build time varies from about 1 to 5 s) and whose transfer stopped at 3,942,474 bytes after 30.1 s with curl exit 56 (receive error, short of the 4,170,240 bytes of the earlier cutout); then HTTP 503 on the next request. Two points follow: transfer time, not the server's build, is the larger part in both runs, and a cutout transfer can end early, which `http_get` handles only by retrying the whole download.
  - **Third run, from a United States host (GitHub Actions runner, Azure `westus3`, 14 Nov 2026 16:31 UTC).** A temporary workflow on the branch `net-check` ran the same read-only requests once (public endpoints, generic User-Agent, nothing stored). Connect time was 0.14 s to IRSA and 0.10 s to S3 (0.27 s from Thailand). One S3 range read of 954,720 bytes took 0.48 s (2.3 s from Thailand), and four in parallel took 0.46 to 0.51 s each, so parallel reads added speed there. The one cutout that succeeded gave its first byte after 0.26 s and all 4,170,240 bytes in 2.5 s at 1.65 MB/s (24.5 s at 0.21 MB/s from Thailand), about ten times faster. Two of the three cutout requests got HTTP 503 after 0.25 to 0.39 s. Counting every cutout request in this recheck, four of seven got 503 (two from Thailand, two from the runner), so 503 is a server-side problem that a good region does not remove. This supports the long-network-path explanation for the slow downloads, from one successful sample per route. It does not show how IRSA behaves under 3 parallel requests or over hundreds of requests. A host in the United States is therefore the better home for the service. Rough estimate, using about 5 s per cutout per worker including retries on 503 (to be measured): one detector (38 to 70 images) takes 1 to 2 minutes, and six detectors (227 to 427 images) take 6 to 12 minutes with 3 workers.
  - **Not tested:** reading the `VARIANCE` rows (the cell store needs them, so a frame costs about 2 MB, not 1 MB), the `WCS-WAVE` table, the same byte offsets on other frames, S3 throttling, and the `qr3` layout. By these numbers, 244 images (Barnard's Star, six detectors) would be about 490 MB, so about 13 to 20 minutes from this connection against about 106 minutes through the cutout service. That is an estimate. The rule question stays open: AGENTS.md names the cutout service, and a partial read of a full frame is the team's decision.

- **2026-11-15, FLAGS bits from the Explanatory Supplement.** Downloaded the supplement (version 2.0, 2026-08-25, 30 MB PDF, one request, into a scratch folder, not kept) and read the flag table, which is Table 16 in this version (Table 10 in the older version the earlier note cited). It gives bit numbers, names and meanings: 0 `TRANSIENT`, 1 `OVERFLOW` (overflow threshold reached during the onboard readout, about half of full-well saturation), 2 `SUR_ERROR`, 6 `NONFUNC`, 7 `DICHROIC`, 9 `MISSING_DATA`, 10 `HOT`, 11 `COLD`, 12 `FULLSAMPLE` (full sample history available; purely informational), 14 `PHANMISS`, 15 `NONLINEAR`, 17 `PERSIST`, 19 `OUTLIER`, 20 `CROSSTALK`, 21 `SOURCE` (a pixel mapped to a known source), 22 `GHOST`, 23 `GHOST_FPA` (unused), 24 `GHOST_EXT`, 25 `STREAK`, 26 `BLOOM`, 27 `SNOWBALL`, 28 `HALO`, 29 `SATELLITE_HALO`. This answers the open bits: **1 is OVERFLOW, 12 is FULLSAMPLE, 26 is BLOOM, 27 is SNOWBALL and 28 is HALO.** The earlier guess that bit 26 was "probably SNOW" was wrong: it is BLOOM, and SNOWBALL is bit 27. Bits 3, 4, 5, 8, 13, 16, 18 and 30 to 31 are not in the table. The supplement has no license or copyright text (a search of the extracted text found none), so its terms are those of IRSA's documentation pages, and we only paraphrase bit names and meanings. Not for a viewer-only scope, but a flags overlay (NASA's optional idea) can use this table.
  - **IRSA incident on 15 Nov.** From about 09:45 to about 09:55 Thailand time IRSA's SIA2 search answered HTTP 502 (about 0.7 s) or timed out for every position tried, including WISE 0855 which had worked a few minutes before. The file server, S3 and the IRSA home page stayed up. A `resolve` run kept retrying (waits of 5 to 30 s) and was stopped by hand, and a probe that asks once every two minutes was used instead. The probe at 09:55 got HTTP 200 and the pre-warm went on. The cause is not known; an incident of ten minutes, after an hour of steady range reads and about 30 requests to the search service, is the kind of thing a pipeline for judges must survive.

  - **IRSA SIA2 search radius (15 Nov).** After the incident above, `resolve` still failed: its search circle of 0.3 degrees around Barnard's Star timed out on every try (180 s each, 10 tries), and a circle of 0.1 degrees timed out at 120 s, while a circle of 0.05 degrees returned 558,710 bytes in 8.9 s and the `fetch` circle of 0.02 degrees takes about 5 s. So the search service has a steep cost above roughly 0.05 to 0.1 degrees. `resolve` now uses 0.05 degrees (`RESOLVE_RADIUS`), which is enough because it only needs the median observing time and the images are 3.5 degrees wide, and it falls back to the middle of DR1 if the search fails. Keep every SIA2 circle at 0.05 degrees or less.
